What you may be experiencing

Sore, itchy bumps that keep coming back?

Scalp breakouts can show up in different ways. You may notice pimples around the hairline, crown or back of the head. They may feel sore when you touch them, itch when you sweat, or sting when you wash or brush your hair.

Sometimes, you may not see any redness or bumps at all. Your scalp may simply feel tender, hot or irritated.

In Singapore, these problems can flare after a sweaty day, exercise, or spending hours in a cap or helmet. Heat and moisture can become trapped against the scalp, and for someone who is already prone to breakouts, that may be enough to trigger another flare-up.

Most people may get an occasional pimple or blackhead on the scalp. These usually settle on their own and do not interfere much with daily life. However, when breakouts persist, spread across the scalp, or appear in recurring patches, it can be difficult to know who to consult. Should you see a dermatologist, or ask your hairstylist?

This is when it helps to look at what may be causing the inflammation. It could be related to heat, sweat, friction, or a new product in your routine. In some cases, hormonal changes can also increase oil production on the scalp. This is particularly common in teenagers going through puberty, although hormonal factors are not the only possible cause.

What’s happening

Scalp breakouts usually start when oil, sweat, dead skin and product residue build up around the hair follicles. When the opening becomes blocked or irritated, the follicle can become inflamed and form a sore or itchy bump.

Singapore’s heat and humidity can make this easier to happen. Sweat and oil build up more quickly, especially after exercise, long commutes or spending hours in a cap or helmet. Styling products, conditioner or other products that are not rinsed away properly can also add to the buildup.

This is why some scalp breakouts seem to settle for a while, then return again.

Not every breakout is caused by the same thing. Some are related to bacteria, while others may involve Malassezia, a yeast that naturally lives on the scalp. When Malassezia is involved, the bumps are often small, similar in size and quite itchy. Seborrheic dermatitis can also occur at the same time and leave the scalp more irritated.

Most scalp breakouts do not cause permanent hair loss. Hair around an inflamed area may shed temporarily and grow back once the scalp settles.

Persistent or deeper inflammation deserves more attention. If the same areas keep becoming inflamed, you notice deeper or painful swellings, or the hair does not grow back after the area has healed, it is worth having the scalp assessed.

Leonica’s diagnostic lens

When scalp breakouts keep returning, the first step is to understand what is driving the inflammation and whether there is anything in your routine that may be making it harder for the scalp to settle.

01
What the breakout actually looks like
Where the bumps are appearing, how inflamed they are, whether they are itchy or painful, and whether the pattern looks like a straightforward scalp breakout or something that may need medical attention.
02
What may be causing it
Oil and sweat may be part of the picture, but bacteria, Malassezia and conditions such as seborrheic dermatitis can produce very similar-looking symptoms. Understanding the likely cause matters because the same treatment will not work for every type of breakout.
03
What is coming into contact with your scalp
Shampoo, conditioner, styling products, scalp products and even the way they are applied or rinsed can affect an already irritated scalp. We look through the routine as a whole rather than focusing on one product in isolation.
04
Heat, sweat and daily habits
Exercise, helmets, caps, tying the hair up for long periods and how soon you wash after sweating can all affect how often the scalp flares.
05
Whether the hair follicles are being affected
Most scalp breakouts settle without affecting hair growth permanently. If we see signs that the inflammation is deeper, keeps returning to the same area, or that hair is not growing back normally, we may recommend seeing a doctor or dermatologist.

For persistent cases, simplifying the routine is often an important part of getting the scalp under control. This may mean temporarily removing products that could be contributing to the irritation and following a consistent scalp-care routine while the inflammation settles.

Frequently asked questions
Is scalp acne the same as acne on my face?

They can look similar because both involve inflammation around a hair follicle, but the scalp is a very different environment. It produces oil, traps heat and sweat easily, and is constantly exposed to shampoo, conditioner and hair products.

So while some of the causes may overlap, scalp breakouts do not always respond in the same way as facial acne.

Why do my scalp breakouts keep coming back?

Usually, something is continuing to trigger or aggravate the scalp.

Heat, sweat, oil, friction from headwear and products left on the scalp can all contribute. Sometimes the breakout may also be related to bacteria, Malassezia or another scalp condition that looks similar to acne.

If it keeps returning despite what you have tried, it is useful to work out what is actually driving the inflammation rather than continuing to experiment with different products.

Can scalp acne cause hair loss?

Most scalp breakouts do not lead to permanent hair loss.

When an area is inflamed, some temporary shedding can happen and the hair usually grows back once the scalp settles. Deeper or long-lasting inflammation can be more concerning, particularly if the same area keeps flaring or the hair does not seem to grow back afterwards.

If you notice this, it is worth having the scalp assessed.

Can I use facial acne products on my scalp?

We would not recommend assuming that a product used for facial acne will be suitable for the scalp.

The scalp can be sensitive, and some acne products may be too irritating or difficult to use properly through the hair. More importantly, not every scalp breakout is caused by the same thing.

If the problem is persistent, it is better to understand what is causing it before adding more products to the scalp.

Should I be washing my hair more often?

If your scalp is oily, sweaty or prone to breakouts, washing your hair with shampoo regularly helps remove sweat, oil, dead skin and product buildup from the scalp.

For most people with an inflamed or breakout-prone scalp, we recommend shampooing daily, especially after heavy sweating. Simply rinsing the hair with water is not the same. The important thing is also to use a suitable shampoo and avoid scratching, which can irritate an already inflamed scalp further.

I was told I have folliculitis. Is that the same thing?

Folliculitis simply means inflammation of the hair follicle, and it can look very similar to what many people describe as scalp acne.

The important part is understanding what is causing the inflammation. Some cases may be related to bacteria, yeast, irritation or other scalp conditions, and the appropriate treatment can differ.

If we suspect an infection or something that requires medical treatment, we will recommend that you see a doctor or dermatologist.
